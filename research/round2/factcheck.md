Checked on 2026-10-22. I read the evidence sections of the five round 2 files and traced 12 figures to their sources. I opened the page where I could. Where a page returned HTTP 403 or sat behind a login, I say so and name the secondary source I used instead. I did not edit any other file.

## 1. Foundation and concealer return at about 23%

Where it is used: returns-economist (Idea 1, the P&L case), inclusion (mentioned, not used as a base), brand-buyer (Shade Twins), daily-habit (Cover Story), contrarian (Still Me).

Primary source: [Eightx beauty and cosmetics return rate benchmarks](https://eightx.co/blog/average-beauty-and-cosmetics-return-rate-benchmarks), a 2026 blog post.

What it says: foundation and concealer "top the list at roughly 23 percent where returns are allowed." The post attributes the figure to "an Eightx synthesis of platform and merchant data, cross-checked against the NRF 2025 Retail Returns Landscape." It states no sample size, no data year and no method. It says the numbers are "aggregated estimates, so treat them as ranges rather than point figures." For corroboration it cites a Pierrine Consulting study that "independently puts colour-cosmetics returns near 22 percent." That figure covers all colour cosmetics, not foundation alone.

Other sources: Tangent.ai repeats the 23% and credits Eightx, so it is a repost and not a second measurement. One search summary credits a later piece with "about 23.4%" citing Rocket Returns (2025), which I did not open. A shade-matching research page reports a range of 4.3% to 22% for beauty and cosmetics overall and says scope and method explain the spread. Eightx's own numbers for beauty overall are 4% to 12%, with a median near 8% to 9%.

Verdict: weaker than claimed. The number is real as a published statement, but nobody has shown the data behind it. It is a conditional rate (only where returns are accepted), and most brands refuse returns on opened makeup. The returns-economist file already rates it low trust, and that rating is correct. The inclusion file is right to refuse it as a base number.

How to use it: write "benchmark estimates put foundation and concealer returns at about 22% to 23% where returns are allowed (Eightx 2026, Pierrine Consulting; method not published)." Do not write it as a measured industry fact. For any money calculation, show the result at 12% and 23% so the reader sees the range.

Better figure: none found. I found no primary retailer dataset. The only defensible alternative is a client's own return-reason data, which a pilot should collect.

## 2. 64% of beauty returns say the product looked different in person

Where it is used: returns-economist (fact 2).

Primary source: the Eightx post above, which attributes it to "Arbelle AI's analysis."

What it says: "Arbelle's analysis puts that at 64 percent of beauty returns." No sample size, no year, no method. The post also says cause shares add up to more than 100% because a return can cite more than one reason. So 64% is the share of returns that cite this cause, not a split.

Verdict: weaker than claimed. It is a vendor statistic with no traceable method. Also, "looked different in person" covers colour, finish, texture and packaging. It is not the same as "wrong shade."

How to use it: either drop it or label it "one vendor analysis, method not published."

A second figure to avoid: a search summary put the share of foundation returns caused by shade at 40% to 60% (a brand blog) and 20% to 65% (attributed to Banuba, via Corso). I could not trace either to a primary study. Both returns-economist and brand-buyer say they found no primary figure for this share. That statement is correct and should stay.

## 3. White cast is a barrier for 25% of skin-of-color respondents against 7%

Where it is used: inclusion (E1, the core of the Veil pitch).

Primary source: "Sunscreen Practices and Preferences of Skin of Color Patients," Journal of Drugs in Dermatology, June 2024 (online 28 May 2024), vol. 23 issue 6, [DOI 10.36849/JDD.8268](https://jddonline.com/articles/dermatology/S1545961624P0456X). I read the journal page and the [Healio report](https://www.healio.com/news/dermatology/20240627/individuals-with-skin-of-color-experience-barriers-to-proper-sunscreen-usage).

What it says: a cross-sectional, questionnaire-based study. White cast concern was reported by 25% of skin-of-color respondents and 7% of non-Hispanic White respondents. Daily use was 25% against 31%. Daily reapplication was 45% against 76% in Healio's report. Other barriers in the abstract: cost 16% against 2%, and lack of knowledge in finding suitable products 41% against 22%.

Sample size and year: Healio gives 150 individuals, 63% skin of color and 37% non-Hispanic White. By my arithmetic that is about 94 and 56 people. The journal page I could reach shows no sample size and no data-collection dates. It lists affiliations at SUNY Downstate, the VA New York Harbor system, Rowan, New York Medical College and St. George's University. I could not confirm that recruitment came from a single medical centre, as the inclusion file states. The inclusion file's claim that the sample is "not national" is safe either way.

Verdict: confirmed, with caveats. The numbers match the abstract. But about 4 in 100 non-Hispanic White respondents and about 24 in 100 skin-of-color respondents is a small, non-random sample, and it measures a stated concern, not behaviour. The largest barrier for skin-of-color respondents in the same abstract is lack of knowledge about suitable products (41%), not white cast (25%). The pitch should not present white cast as the main barrier.

Better evidence for the white cast mechanism: a 2025 PLOS One study ([PMC12380271](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12380271/)) of 13 women, Fitzpatrick II to VI, sponsored by Good Molecules with all authors employed by the sponsor. It used the score (L*f minus L*i) divided by (100 minus L*i) at 2 mg/cm2. Zinc oxide at 20% and 30% was "unacceptable" for all volunteers. Visible cast appeared at 10% zinc oxide on tan and brown subtypes and at 30% on light subtypes. The authors conclude that cast "manifests differently across skin tones but can be equally undesirable." That is a small, sponsor-run study. It supports "cast is real and measurable," and it does not show that cast drives non-use.

## 4. 61% of Black respondents never wear sunscreen

Where it is used: inclusion (E2, in the Veil business case).

Source chain: the figure is attributed to a 2020 Consumer Reports survey. I could not reach the Consumer Reports original. I found three secondary sources that agree. [Cronkite News, 26 April 2023](https://cronkitenews.azpbs.org/2023/04/26/new-sunscreens-people-of-color/): "61% of Blacks and 23% of Latinos said they never wear sunscreen, Consumer Reports found in a 2020 survey." [The Daily Beast, 28 May 2021](https://www.thedailybeast.com/why-are-sunscreen-ads-ignoring-and-failing-people-of-color/): "a Consumer Reports survey of 2007 people" (probably 2,007). A PMC review repeats it. A separate framing of the same survey says only 39% of African Americans ever use sunscreen against 77% of Latinos and whites, which is the same fact seen from the other side.

Sample size and year: 2,007 people per the Daily Beast, 2020 per Cronkite. I could not confirm the survey method, the Black subsample size or the question wording. A Consumer Reports page I opened ("Sunscreen for Dark Skin Is a Must") does not contain the figure.

Verdict: confirmed as a widely reported figure, with the primary source unverified. The subsample is probably a few hundred people at most, so treat it as a rough size, not a precise rate.

Two risks in the inclusion file's use. First, the Veil business case lists the 25% white cast figure, then the 61% never-wear figure, then Mintel's 84%, in one sentence. A reader will infer that white cast causes non-use. No source shows that. The 2020 survey did not ask about white cast as far as I can see, and the study in figure 3 measured concern, not behaviour. Keep the claims apart. Second, a different "61%" exists in the Benchmarking Company shade survey (61% of participants with darker skin tones have abandoned a brand over a non-inclusive shade range). Do not mix the two.

Related figure: Mintel's "84% of Black sunscreen users say there are not enough choices for their skin tones" is only traceable to the Cronkite article (April 2023, undated report, "last month") and a PMC review. I could not reach Mintel. Treat it as confirmed secondary. The same Mintel coverage says the US sun protection market is about $1.8 billion.

Better figure: a 2010 study of 2,187 California African American adults found 63% never used sunscreen (surfaced in a search summary; I did not open it). It is old but peer-reviewed and has a larger Black sample, and it agrees with the 61%.

## 5. Shade finding is hard: 41% overall, 56% on darker skin tones

Where it is used: brand-buyer (evidence 4, Shade Twins), daily-habit (evidence 8, Cover Story).

Primary source: [The Benchmarking Company, "Shade (Mis)Match and Inclusivity," 2026 infographic series #1](https://benchmarkingcompany.com/news/shade-mismatch-inclusivity-2026-infographic-series-1/).

What it says: more than 4,300 US females, fielded March 2026, published 28 April 2026. Respondents rated how hard it is to find their match as "somewhat to very difficult." For facial complexion products the figure is 41% overall and 56% for the darker group (Monk Skin Tone 6 to 10). Other rows: self-tanner 38%, body makeup 38% (44% darker), lip colour 19%, eye shadow 10%. The page does not say who commissioned the survey.

Source-chain problems in the files. Brand-buyer cites CosmeticOBS. The CosmeticOBS page I fetched reports the same survey and numbers, so it is the same data in a different outlet, not a second source. Daily-habit cites a BeautyMatter article for the same numbers. I fetched that article and it does not contain the 41% or 56% figures. It quotes an older NPD Group study for Make Up For Ever (73% find it challenging, 54% say shade is the top challenge) and a Shiseido internal study (94% wear the wrong shade), both undated and with no sample size. The daily-habit URL should be replaced with the Benchmarking Company page.

Verdict: confirmed. The sample is large and recent. Caveats: the 56% is the Monk 6 to 10 subgroup, not Fitzpatrick IV to VI, and the page's skin-tone groups overlap (Fitzpatrick 4 to 6 is 17% of respondents and Monk 5 to 10 is 25%). The sample is of US females, not US shoppers who buy foundation. The page gives no sample size for the darker subgroup.

Better figures from the same page, which hit the Round 2 ideas harder:
- 39% of all participants have abandoned a brand because they felt its shade range was not inclusive, and 61% of participants with darker skin tones said the same.
- Shade mixing to reach a match (two or more shades): 61% for Monk 1 to 3, 69% for Monk 4 to 5, 79% for Monk 6 to 7, 72% for Monk 8 to 10. This supports the Seam blend idea directly.
- 80% change complexion shade seasonally, 71% buy another shade to manage this, and 53% mix shades to match changing skin. This supports the seasonal re-match repeat use in Neck Test and Seam.
- How people find a shade: 66% trial and error, 59% in-store matching, 40% online quiz, 38% guess from the description.
The page has no return or refund figures.

## 6. 54% of foundation buyers bought more than one product because they were unsure of the shade

Where it is used: inclusion (E7, Seam), returns-economist (bracketing context).

Primary source: [Ipsos, US, 9 June 2016](https://www.ipsos.com/en-us/node/134791), "an online survey among women conducted by Ipsos on behalf of Boots Retail, USA Inc."

What it says: 54% of women who bought foundation also bought more than one product because they were unsure of the shade. Women bought about 2.4 shades of the same foundation in the last three months. 65% say they become frustrated finding the shade.

Sample size and year: not stated on the page (the full method is in a downloadable PDF I could not open). Fieldwork dates are not stated. The release is dated 2016.

Verdict: confirmed on the page, weaker than claimed in use. The inclusion file writes "bottle" in its summary. The page says "product." The study is ten years old, it was commissioned by a retailer, and the sample size is unknown. It supports the bracketing behaviour, not the size of the problem today.

Better figure: the 2026 Benchmarking Company data in figure 5 covers the same behaviour with a stated sample (more than 4,300) and a current date.

## 7. Virtual try-on lifts conversion (Avon +320%, 2.5 to 3.2 times, Cosnova 2.5 times)

Where it is used: returns-economist (fact 9), inclusion (E10), brand-buyer (evidence 8 to 10), daily-habit (evidence 15).

Vendor sources: [Avon](https://www.perfectcorp.com/business/successstory/Avon-Sees-Boost-in-Conversion-Perfect-Corp-Virtual-Try-On) and the other Perfect Corp case studies. These are self-reported, compare try-on users with non-users, and publish no baseline or method. All five files already flag this. The inclusion file also notes that the Avon page says nothing about returns.

Independent source: Tan, Chandukala and Reddy, ["Augmented Reality in Retail and Its Impact on Sales," Journal of Marketing 86(1), 48 to 66, January 2022](https://doi.org/10.1177/0022242921995449). I read the [Kellogg Insight summary](https://insight.kellogg.northwestern.edu/article/selling-niche-products-augmented-reality) and the [AMA press release](https://www.ama.org/press-releases/press-release-from-the-journal-of-marketing-augmented-reality-in-retail-and-its-impact-on-sales/). The data come from an unnamed international cosmetics retailer with a mobile app that offered AR try-on for eyeshadow, eyeliner, lipstick and lip gloss. The sample is 160,400 customers in one Asia Pacific market, 2,300 products and more than 800,000 shopping sessions (about 20% with AR), over 19 months from December 2017 to June 2019. Descriptively, AR users browsed about 20% longer, viewed nearly 30% more products and had a purchase rate nearly 20% higher. But the authors state that the causal impact on sales "appears to be small." It is larger for less popular brands, narrower-appeal products, more expensive products and customers new to the online channel or category.

I did not read the full paper, so I cannot give the causal effect size or the estimation method. The abstract pages do not state them.

Verdict: weaker than claimed. The independent evidence supports a positive but small effect on sales, concentrated where shoppers are uncertain. It does not support multiples of 2.5 to 4. The descriptive 20% gap is itself an upper bound, because people who open an AR feature are more likely to buy anyway.

How to use it: replace "try-on users convert 2.5 to 3.2 times as often" with "an independent study of a 160,400-customer cosmetics retailer found a small positive sales effect from AR try-on, strongest for niche, expensive products and new shoppers (Tan et al., Journal of Marketing, 2022)." This also supports the pitch angle that tools help most when uncertainty is high, which fits shade matching.

## 8. Virtual try-on reduces returns (8% fewer returns, YouCam for Web)

Where it is used: returns-economist (fact 9), inclusion (E10 notes no returns data), brand-buyer (implied), daily-habit.

Vendor source: the [Business Wire release for YouCam for Web, 1 April 2020](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20200401005488/en/Perfect-Corp.-Partners-with-Jillian-Dempsey-for-the-Launch-of-YouCam-for-Web-AR-Try-On-Solution). I did not re-open it. Both the claim and its source are from the sponsor.

Independent evidence I found, none of it showing that try-on cuts beauty returns:
- Tan et al. (figure 7): returns were not reported in the summaries I read.
- A 2026 paper in the International Journal of Research in Marketing, ["The virtual idealization paradox: how augmented reality previews increase product returns through elevated expectations"](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0167811626000583). Page returned HTTP 403. A search summary describes four studies (one lab and three online experiments) and says that after product failure, AR increases return behaviour relative to image previews, because it inflates expectations. This is secondhand, and these are lab and online experiments, not retailer data. It points the wrong way for the returns claim.
- A 2026 meta-analysis in the Journal of Retailing (ScienceDirect S0022435926000801, HTTP 403), reported through a search summary only. It says the prepurchase effect of AR and VR was weaker under lenient return policies than strict ones (r about .164 against .374), and that effects on post-purchase outcomes were stronger for online-only retailers. I could not read it, so I cannot give the post-purchase effect size.
- A Marketing Science Institute recap says AR may improve choices and reduce returns, but that shoppers who receive a product that falls short may blame the brand and buy less. No figures are public.
- A European Marketing Academy 2025 paper (Hayati, Chen, van Everdingen) says the effect of AR on returns "remains unclear."
- Search summaries of "64% fewer returns," "30% fewer returns" and similar come from vendor blogs with no method.

Verdict: not supported by independent evidence. I found no peer-reviewed field study showing that cosmetic try-on reduces returns, and the one experimental paper I found points toward higher returns. The returns-economist file says it could not find an independent A/B test of try-on on returns. That finding is correct, and I could not improve on it.

How to use it: do not claim that try-on lowers returns. The safe claim is that a measured, closed-loop shade proof (Neck Test, Seam) addresses the uncertainty that drives returns, and that a pilot must measure the effect. Neck Test's own 20% return-reduction figure is labelled an assumption to test, which is the right framing.

## 9. Fitzpatrick V and VI are 5.8% of dermatology images

Where it is used: inclusion (E6, as the reliability caveat for deep skin), daily-habit and contrarian (indirectly).

Primary source: [arXiv 2601.00840, "A Global Atlas of Digital Dermatology to Map Innovation and Disparities" (SkinMap), v2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.00840). The abstract says the atlas contains "more than 1.1 million images of skin conditions" and that "Darker skin tones (Fitzpatrick V-VI) constitute only 5.8% of images."

Sample size and year: more than 1.1 million images, unified from public dermatology datasets. It is a January 2026 preprint, not peer reviewed as far as I can tell.

Verdict: confirmed as stated. One scope caveat. The figure describes public disease-image datasets used by dermatology research. It says nothing about the training data behind YouCam's skin analysis, which is proprietary. The inclusion file words this carefully ("the data that skin AI learns from"). Keep that wording and do not say "YouCam's model has 5.8% deep-skin training data."

Peer-reviewed support that I found:
- Wen et al., "Characteristics of publicly available skin cancer image datasets: a systematic review," Lancet Digital Health, January 2022 (4(1): e64 to e74). It identified 21 open-access datasets with 106,950 images. Fitzpatrick skin type was recorded for 2,236 images (2.1%), and ethnicity for 1,415 (1.3%). Among images with ethnicity recorded, none came from people of African, Afro-Caribbean or South Asian background. Search summaries say only 10 images were brown skin and 1 was dark brown or black, among 2,436 with skin colour stated. I read the abstract figures through a secondary summary, not the paper.
- Groh et al., Fitzpatrick 17k (CVPR workshop 2021): 2,168 of 16,577 images (about 13%, or about 13.5% of the 16,012 with a known label, my arithmetic) are Fitzpatrick V and VI.

Better figure: the Wen 2022 finding that only 2.1% of images carry a skin-type label at all is stronger for the Round 2 argument. It means deep-skin performance often cannot be audited, not just that deep skin is rare.

## 10. Sunscreen market size: $13.5B (2024), $14.4B (2025); tinted sunscreen $1.08B

Where it is used: inclusion (E15, Veil business case).

What I found: published estimates differ by about 2.5 times because of scope.
- Global Market Insights, suncare products: $13.4B in 2024, $13.9B in 2025.
- Research and Markets, suncare: $14.42B in 2024 (3.1% CAGR 2019 to 2024).
- Arizton, sun care products: $14.30B in 2024.
- TechSci Research: about $14.45B in 2024.
- GlobalData: $12B in 2023.
- Statista (via a mirror site, unverified): $11.52B in 2024.
- Meticulous Research: $17.8B in 2024 and $19.2B in 2025.
- QYResearch, sunscreening products only: $6.8B in 2024.

I did not find a source for exactly "$13.5B in 2024" and "$14.4B in 2025." The nearest matches are $13.4B (Global Market Insights, 2024) and $14.42B, which Research and Markets assigns to 2024, not 2025. So the inclusion file likely mislabels the year on the second number.

Tinted sunscreen: [Straits Research](https://straitsresearch.com/report/tinted-sunscreen-market) gives $1,080.4 million for the 2025 base year, $1,140.9 million for 2026 and $1,764.25 million by 2034 (5.6% CAGR). Confirmed as stated. The page shows no methodology, and I found no second source for the tinted segment.

US figure: Mintel, quoted by Cronkite News, describes a $1.8 billion US sun protection market, undated.

Verdict: weaker than claimed. The order of magnitude (low double-digit billions globally) is safe. The year labels are wrong, and one decimal place is false precision.

How to use it: write "global suncare is estimated at $13B to $15B (2024, several research firms, definitions differ)." Cite Mintel's $1.8B if a US figure is needed. Do not use the market size as evidence of impact for the Veil idea. A large market says nothing about how many people the cast problem blocks.

## 11. Skin-of-color market size: "almost 90%" of ethnic hair and beauty spend

Where it is used: inclusion (E15).

Primary source: [Nielsen press release, 12 September 2019](https://www.nielsen.com/us/en/press-releases/2019/african-american-spending-power-demands-that-marketers-show-more-love-and-support-for-black-culture/), from the Diverse Intelligence Series report "It's in the Bag." The wording is: "African Americans dominate the ethnic hair and beauty aids category, accounting for almost 90% of the overall spend." No dollar figure, no data year and no sample size are stated on the page.

Verdict: confirmed as a quote, weaker than claimed in use. It describes a narrow category (ethnic hair and beauty aids, which is mostly hair care), and it says nothing about sunscreen or complexion products. It is also seven years old.

Better figure: McKinsey, "Black representation in the beauty industry" (2022). Black Americans spent about $6.6 billion on beauty in 2021, 11.1% of the US beauty market, slightly under their 12.4% population share. Black-founded or Black-owned brands hold about 2.5% of beauty revenue (BeautyMatter gives 2.4%). Black consumers are three times more likely than non-Black consumers to be dissatisfied with their options in hair care, skin care and makeup. McKinsey sizes the gap at a $2.6 billion opportunity. The basis is a survey of 7,400 Black beauty consumers (September 2021) plus executive interviews. I confirmed these through [Chain Drug Review](https://digitaledition.chaindrugreview.com/articles/better-serving-black-shoppers-is-a-2-6-billion-opportunity) and Retail Brew search summaries, not the McKinsey page itself. This is a stronger and more current basis for a skin-of-color impact claim than the Nielsen quote, and it is not specific to sunscreen.

## 12. Returns baseline: NRF 15.8% and 19.3%; Pitney Bowes 21% of order value

Where it is used: all five files.

NRF: [NRF press release, 15 October 2025](https://nrf.com/media-center/press-releases/consumers-expected-to-return-nearly-850-billion-in-merchandise-in-2025). Confirmed. Total returns are forecast at 15.8% of annual sales ($849.9B, down from 16.9% and $890B), online returns at 19.3% of online sales, and holiday returns at 17%. The survey was run by NRF and Happy Returns (a UPS company) in summer 2025, with 2,006 consumers who returned at least one online purchase in the past 12 months and 358 ecommerce professionals at US merchants with more than $500 million in revenue. The inclusion file's "358 e-commerce professionals" is correct. Note that 15.8% is a forecast, not a measured outcome. Brand-buyer cites Appriss ($706B actually returned in 2025) as a check. I did not open that source.

Pitney Bowes: the 21% figure comes from the [Pitney Bowes BOXpoll, 14 April 2022](https://www.businesswire.com/news/home/20220414005151/en/Pitney-Bowes-Survey-Returns-Cost-US-Online-Retailers-21-of-Order-Value) (Business Wire page returned HTTP 403, so I confirmed it through the search summary and an [eMarketer article from April 2022](https://www.emarketer.com/content/online-returns-eat-retailers-bottom-lines)). Returns cost medium and large digital and omnichannel brands an average of 21% of order value. The returns-economist file lists the publication year as unconfirmed. It is 2022. I did not see the sample size.

Verdict: confirmed, with dating caveats. The NRF figures are all-category averages and say nothing about beauty. The Pitney Bowes figure is four years old and measures processing cost across categories. For a cosmetic that cannot be resold, the true cost may be higher, but that is an operator inference, not a sourced fact.

## Summary for the ideas

1. Neck Test and Seam rest on the 23% return figure. It is an untraced vendor estimate. Keep the ideas, but present the return reduction as a pilot hypothesis with a range, not a forecast. Their stronger evidence is the 2026 Benchmarking Company data: 41% and 56% have trouble, 39% and 61% have left a brand over its shade range, and 61% to 79% mix shades.
2. Veil has real data on white cast but keep the claims separate. Use 25% against 7% (a concern, n of about 150), the PLOS One cast measurements (n=13, sponsor-run) and the 61% never-wear figure (secondary source) as three separate points. Do not link them causally. Also note that lack of knowledge of suitable products (41%) outranks white cast (25%) in the same journal abstract.
3. Any claim that try-on lifts conversion should cite Tan et al. (small, positive, stronger under uncertainty) and not the Perfect Corp multiples. Any claim that it cuts returns has no independent support, and at least one experimental paper suggests the opposite.
4. The deep-skin reliability caveat is well supported. Use the Wen 2022 point that only 2.1% of skin cancer images carry a Fitzpatrick label alongside the 5.8%.
5. Market sizes should be quoted as ranges, with the definition, and should not stand as impact evidence.
